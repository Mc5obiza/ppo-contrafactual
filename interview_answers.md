# Interview Answers — PPO-Guided Latent Diffusion Counterfactuals for Chest Radiography

## 1. Motivation and Problem Framing

**1. Why RL-guided diffusion instead of Grad-CAM directly on the classifier?**
Grad-CAM gives you a saliency map — where the classifier is looking. It doesn't give you a counterfactual — an actual image showing what the pathology would look like absent or present. A counterfactual has to be a valid image (on-manifold, generated by a model that knows what chest X-rays look like), has to actually flip the classifier's decision, and has to preserve everything about the patient that isn't the pathology. That's three simultaneous objectives, which is why I used a generative model (SD1.5 + LoRA) for image validity and a guided sampling process for the flip + preservation trade-off. PPO comes in because the guidance schedule — how much classifier pressure to apply, at which timestep, with what preservation counter-pressure — is itself a sequential decision problem where early choices affect what's achievable later, and I wanted that schedule to be learned rather than hand-tuned per image.

**2. What does "counterfactual validity" actually mean here, and why is it weaker than "this system explains disease"?**
`counterfactual_validity()` in `metrics.py` is purely a function of the classifier: it checks that the score moved by at least τ=0.8 in the target direction AND crossed the 0.5 decision boundary. That's it. It says nothing about whether the *pixels that changed* are pixels a radiologist would agree are diagnostic. This is exactly the gap the held-out validation exposed: a system can score CV=0.995 by learning an adversarial perturbation against the one classifier that's grading it, with zero requirement that the change be semantically about pneumonia. CV measures "did I convince this specific function," not "did I depict this specific pathology."

**3. Why RSNA pneumonia, and why binary?**
[Exact reasoning for corpus/task selection not found explicitly in context beyond "RSNA data ingestion" being the given starting point — likely inherited as the assigned project scope rather than a choice I made.] The binary framing (normal vs. pneumonia) simplifies the guidance objective to a single scalar target `c ∈ {0,1}` and a single BCE loss (`L_cls` in `generate_counterfactual`), which keeps the action space and reward function tractable. The cost is that a binary classifier trained on a single corpus has a small, easily-saturated decision surface — which is exactly what motivated the later ensemble-classifier work (multi-pathology models are "much less able to rely on a single global shortcut, since that shortcut wouldn't help distinguish between conditions" — `xrv_classifier.py` docstring).

**4. Unpack "the most dangerous metric is the one you optimised against."**
The PPO reward function's flip terms (`R_hit`, `R_stop`, weighted via `LAM_HIT`/`LAM_STOP`) are direct functions of `d_next = |f(x_next) - c|`, where `f` is the guidance classifier. CV is *also* a direct function of that same `f`. So the metric I report as "success" and the signal I optimise are computed from the identical function. Any direction in which the classifier is exploitable — any shortcut, any brittleness — is a direction the policy will find and exploit, because nothing in the reward distinguishes "found a real pneumonia pattern" from "found an adversarial pattern this classifier is sensitive to." The only way to catch that is a metric computed from a function that was never in the optimisation loop — which is exactly what the held-out validators (`-chex`, `-nih`, later `-nih`/`-pc`/`-mimic_ch`) are for.

**5. Why wasn't validation (WP6) part of the original plan?**
The original plan was to build the pipeline and report the primary metric suite (CV/FID/IP/CTR/SCP) as the deliverable — which is what Chapter 4 does up through Table `tab:results` (mean CV 0.975). The held-out validation was added mid-internship once I asked whether that number would survive an independent check, and it didn't (CV 0.995→as low as 0.000 on `-nih`). Once that was known, validating the countermeasures I built in response became the load-bearing part of the rest of the internship — the report says directly that WP6's results "are the central finding of this report."

## 2. RL / PPO Formulation

**6. Walk through the 9-dim action space.**
From `environment.py` `step()`, all 9 raw logits go through squashing transforms:
- `action[0]` → `strength_t = 0.2 + 0.5*sigmoid(·)` ∈ [0.2, 0.7]: fraction of the DDIM trajectory used for SDEdit-style re-noising — how much of the original image's structure gets overwritten before denoising starts.
- `action[1]` → `cls_scale_t = 0.3 + 0.9*sigmoid(·)` ∈ [0.3, 1.2]: multiplier on classifier-guidance strength.
- `action[2]` → `pres_scale_t = 0.3 + 1.2*sigmoid(·)` ∈ [0.3, 1.5]: multiplier on preservation-guidance strength (the counter-pressure keeping the image anatomically faithful).
- `action[3]` → `tau_t = sigmoid(·)`: a "stop" signal gating `R_stop`.
- `action[4]`, `action[5]`: two booleans (`> 0.5`) each adding one extra refinement pass on top of `config.ENV_REFINEMENT_PASSES`.
- `action[6]` → `strength_boost = 0.85 + 0.30*sigmoid(·)`: multiplies `strength_t` per chained pass, growing with `pass_idx`.
- `action[7]` → `guidance_bias = 0.70 + 0.60*sigmoid(·)` ∈ [0.70, 1.30]: shifts the cls/pres balance — multiplies `cls_scale` up and divides `pres_scale` (via `inv_guidance_bias`) simultaneously.
- `action[8]` → `ramp_start_frac_t = 0.1 + 0.4*sigmoid(·)` ∈ [0.1, 0.5]: where in the DDIM trajectory guidance starts ramping from 0 to full strength.

These combine per-pass into `pass_strength`, `pass_cls_scale`, `pass_pres_scale` fed to `generator.generate_counterfactual`.

**7. Why cap `strength_t` at 1.2 instead of 3.0?**
At the old ceiling (`0.5 + 2.5*sigmoid`, max 3.0), a single step could apply enough SDEdit re-noising strength to brute-force the classifier flip in one shot. Combined with the score-only stop criterion at the time, that collapsed episodes to `avg_ep_len=1.00` — the MDP became a one-shot contextual bandit, and the value head, GAE, and the 25-step budget had literally nothing to do. Narrowing the ceiling to 1.2 forces the flip to require several steps of accumulated push, which is what gives PPO an actual credit-assignment problem to learn from.

**8. Observation vector and the conv trunk.**
`get_observation()` concatenates `z_orig` (4096-d flattened latent), `z_t` (4096-d), step fraction `s_t`, `d_t = |f(x_t)-c|`, target class `c`, and the flattened 256×256 lung mask `m_t` — 73,731 dims total. The MLP trunk saw all of that as one unstructured vector. The conv trunk (`_build_conv_trunk`) reshapes `z_orig`, `z_t`, and a pooled mask back into their native (C,32,32) spatial grids, runs a small 3-layer conv stack + adaptive pool, and concatenates the resulting 128-d feature with the 3 scalars before a 2-layer MLP head. This exploits the fact that 3 of the 6 observation blocks are actually spatial grids the MLP trunk was treating as flat noise. Both trunks are kept (`config.POLICY_TRUNK`) so archived MLP-trained policies stay loadable.

**9. What is `direction_scale`?**
`direction_scale = 2.25 if target_c == 1.0 else 1.0` — a 2.25× multiplier applied to the whole reward for the n→p direction, meant to give that harder direction extra learning signal. Originally it only scaled the "push toward target" terms (`R_prog`, `R_val`, `R_close2`, `R_hit`, `R_stop`) and left `R_fid`/`R_pres` unscaled, which made preservation relatively *less* important specifically on the direction already producing the worst diffuse edits (81% of pixels changed). I fixed it to apply uniformly to every term. My own code comment notes that under PPO+GAE's advantage normalization — advantages are renormalized to zero-mean/unit-std per update in `compute_gae` — a uniform scalar on the whole reward washes out almost entirely, so the fix removes the *relative* re-weighting bug but doesn't actually give n→p extra learning signal the way it was originally intended to. If n→p still lags after retraining, the fix is training hyperparameters for that policy specifically, not reward scaling.

**10. The `shaped_rewards`/`DELTA_MIX` dead-code bug.**
`compute_gae()` computes `shaped_rewards` — a tau-gated blend of raw reward and the GAE advantage, controlled by `config.DELTA_MIX=0.2` — and returns it. But `PPOTrainer.update()` was unpacking that return value and never using it: `L_value` was computed as `F.mse_loss(values, returns)`, using the raw GAE bootstrap target instead. So `DELTA_MIX` was computed every single update and had exactly zero effect on training the whole time it existed. I found it by reading `update()` line by line against what `compute_gae()` actually returns and noticing `shaped_rewards` never appeared on the right-hand side of anything. Fixed by using `shaped_rewards` as the value-regression target instead of `returns`, which is the standard place a shaped-reward signal is meant to land.

**11. Why does `is_valid` require both score AND budget conditions?**
With the score condition alone (`d_next.item() < EPSILON_STOP`), a diffuse whole-image wash that fools the classifier satisfies validity in a single step — that's the `avg_ep_len=1.00` collapse from Q7. Adding `budget_ok = edit_l1 < EDIT_BUDGET_L1` forces the flip to also be *cheap*, i.e. achieved with a small edit, which is what forces the policy to iteratively refine rather than brute-force in one pass. Note `EDIT_BUDGET_L1` is currently set back to `inf` (reverted 2026-08-07) alongside `BUDGET_T` reverting from 8 back to 25 — so this gate exists in the code and reward but isn't currently binding at its configured value; the real per-step constraint on edit magnitude comes from `strength_t`'s narrowed ceiling instead.

**12. What is `FIXED_EPISODE_LENGTH`?**
Under this flag, `done` in `step()` keys purely off `current_step >= self.T` (25 steps), and `is_valid` no longer terminates the episode early. Two reasons: (1) episode cost becomes exactly `BUDGET_T` steps regardless of how "lucky" the policy gets, which is what makes a training run plannable against the 24h SLURM walltime cap; (2) `R_hit`/`R_stop` are per-step terms, so ending on first validity turned the flip bonus into a terminal payout the policy could collect once and quit on — with no early exit, those terms accrue for every step validity is *held*, which is closer to the intended semantics. My own code comment is explicit this is NOT a speed optimization — job 17653310 logged `avg_ep_len=8.00` on every update even before this change, meaning `is_valid` simply never fired and episodes already ran the full budget; `avg_return` is not comparable across the flag change, so I use `frac_valid` from the per-update log line instead.

**13. The actor-saturation collapse.**
In v1, guidance diagnostics (serialised per policy checkpoint) measured `guidance_bias` at 1.2976±0.0054 for n→p and 0.7005±0.0012 for p→n — the two extreme ends of its (0.70, 1.30) sigmoid range, with standard deviation near zero despite the policy sampling with exploration noise std≈0.61. That means the actor's logits had drifted to roughly ±5.5, where `sigmoid` is flat — the squash absorbed all exploration noise, and the policy was effectively deterministic and state-independent on that dimension. I added `L_act_sat = F.relu(logits.abs() - ACT_SAT_THRESHOLD).pow(2).mean()` (threshold 3.0, weight `LAM_ACT_SAT=1e-2`) — a hinge that only penalizes the logit magnitude beyond the threshold, leaving the responsive region of the sigmoid untouched while pulling the dead tail back toward the sensitive zone.

**14. The auxiliary feasibility/progress/stop heads.**
`feasibility_head`, `progress_head`, `stop_head` are separate linear heads off the shared trunk, each producing an auxiliary prediction trained alongside the main actor/critic. In `update()`: `L_feas` is BCE against `feas_labels = (returns > 0).float()`; `L_prog_aux` is MSE against `td_targets` (the one-step TD target); `L_stop_aux` is BCE against `stop_labels = (tau > 0.5)`. They're not used at inference (`get_action_and_value` returns them but the action only comes from the actor logits) — they exist purely as auxiliary training losses (`LAM_FEAS_AUX`, `LAM_PROG_AUX`, `LAM_STOP_AUX`, each 0.1) added into `L_total`, the standard auxiliary-task regularization pattern for shaping the shared trunk's representation with extra supervised signal beyond the sparse PPO objective.

**15. Why two separate policies instead of one target-conditioned policy?**
[Not explicitly justified in context beyond the report calling it out as a limitation.] Practically it's simpler: each policy only ever sees one target class, so the environment, reward, and observation don't need a target-conditioning mechanism, and each direction's checkpoint/guidance-diagnostics are independent. The cost, stated directly in the report, is that a single target-conditioned policy with a randomized per-episode target would let the harder n→p direction "borrow capacity" from the easier p→n direction — but that requires an environment refactor I didn't undertake in this internship.

**16. Reward decomposition and v1→v2 rebalance.**
- `R_prog = d_t - d_next` (did this step reduce distance-to-target)
- `R_val = -d_next` (absolute closeness to target)
- `R_close2 = -d_next²` (quadratic closeness — extra pressure near convergence)
- `R_fid = -edit_l1` (L1 penalty on total pixel change vs. original)
- `R_pres = -(seg_term + ETA_OBJ * kl_obj)` (segmentation-weighted L1 change inside the lung + KL divergence of YOLO object-detector features, both penalizing preservation violations)
- `R_bud = -1.0` (flat per-step cost, encourages finishing sooner when combined with terminal bonuses)
- `R_hit = float(is_valid)`, `R_stop = float(is_valid) if tau>0.5 else 0` (flip bonuses)
- `R_sparsity`: annealed extra L1 penalty, optionally weighted outside the CAM region only

Weights before (v1, inferred from the change-log commentary) had `LAM_HIT`/`LAM_STOP` around 6/8, `LAM_PRES_R=0.5`. After the rebalance (current `config.py`): `LAM_PROG=1.5, LAM_VAL=2.0, LAM_CLOSE2=2.5, LAM_FID=2.0, LAM_PRES_R=3.0, LAM_BUD=0.1, LAM_HIT=2.0, LAM_STOP=2.0`. Before the rebalance, the flip terms (`hit`+`stop`) made up 93% of the total reward mass; the target for the rebalance was roughly 40% flip / 40% preservation / 20% progress, verified via the `_format_reward_split` diagnostic printed every update.

**17. Why L1 instead of L2 for `R_fid`?**
L2/MSE prefers many small changes over few large ones — it actively rewards exactly the diffuse whole-thorax editing the rebalance exists to remove, because spreading a fixed total squared error over more pixels lowers the sum. L1 is the sparsity-inducing norm — it's indifferent to whether the same total change is concentrated or spread out, which stops rewarding diffuseness. The magnitude changed materially in switching (MSE was ≈0.0033 where L1 is ≈0.04), which is why `LAM_FID` moved from 0.5 to 2.0 to keep the term's contribution to total reward comparable.

**18. CV safeguard / ramp-freeze mechanism.**
`_lambda_for_update` anneals `lambda_sparsity` from 0 to `LAM_SPARSITY_TARGET=2.0` linearly (or cosine) over a fractional window (`SPARSITY_RAMP_START_FRAC=0.2` to `SPARSITY_RAMP_END_FRAC=0.6` of total updates). During that window, `_apply_cv_safeguard` periodically measures held-out CV (`_measure_holdout_cv`, using the same criterion as `metrics.counterfactual_validity`, τ=0.8/boundary=0.5) on genuinely held-out far-pool images the policy never trains on. If CV drops more than `CV_DROP_TOLERANCE=0.08` below the pre-ramp baseline, `lambda_frozen` is set to `lambda_last_ok` — the last lambda value that PASSED a probe, not the current one, since the current value is what caused the drop — and held there for the rest of training. It's deliberately latched rather than allowed to un-freeze if CV recovers, because letting a recovering CV resume the ramp would make `lambda_sparsity` oscillate and the whole run uninterpretable — you'd never know which lambda produced which behavior.

**19. PPO hyperparameters.**
`GAMMA=0.99`, `GAE_LAMBDA=0.95`, `PPO_CLIP=0.2`, `LR=3e-4`, `C_VALUE=0.5`, `C_ENTROPY=0.01` are all standard PPO defaults from the original paper/common implementations, not values I ablated — [whether any were specifically tuned for this task is not found in context; they read as sensible off-the-shelf choices given the compute budget]. `episodes_per_update=8` and `n_epochs=4` (inner PPO epochs per update) are set in the `train()`/`update()` call signatures. `N_EPISODES=800` total is explicitly documented in the report as "a conservative placeholder sized for the available walltime, not a value established by ablation."

**20. Why `_format_reward_split` normalizes by sum of absolute values.**
The reward terms have mixed signs (progress/hit/stop are typically positive contributions, fid/pres/bud are negative penalties). If you normalized by the *signed* total, a large positive term and a large negative term could cancel and report a misleadingly small denominator, making individual term shares blow up or look meaningless. What actually matters for diagnosing the rebalance is how much each term is *pulling*, regardless of direction — so the share is `|term| / sum(|terms|)`, which is the metric that verified the v1→v2 fix actually landed (93% flip share → ~40%).

## 3. Diffusion Guidance Mechanics

**21. Classifier guidance and the two-backward-pass design.**
At each DDIM step with `guidance_weight > 0`, I take the UNet's predicted clean latent `z0_hat`, decode it to pixel space, run it through the frozen classifier to get `L_cls = BCE(f(x_hat), c)`, and compute `grad_cls = ∂L_cls/∂z0_hat` via `torch.autograd.grad`. Separately I compute `L_pres_g = seg_term + ETA_OBJ*obj_term` (preservation loss) and its own gradient `grad_pres`. These are combined as `action = -guidance_weight*(ALPHA_CLS*1.15*cls_scale)*grad_cls - guidance_weight*(ALPHA_PRES*pres_scale)*grad_pres`, masked by `edit_mask`, and added to `z_t`. I run two separate `torch.autograd.grad` calls (the first with `retain_graph=True`) rather than backpropagating one weighted sum, because the two loss terms need independently-scaled gradients — the policy's `cls_scale`/`pres_scale` actions scale them by different, runtime-determined factors that aren't known until after the classifier and preservation forward passes.

**22. Why not fuse the two backwards, and the fp16 argument.**
This is the single most expensive operation in the pipeline — it runs on 60-80% of the DDIM steps of every refinement pass of every PPO step, and `retain_graph=True` means the VAE decoder gets walked backward twice per guided step. It looks trivially fusable: `action`'s coefficients (`ALPHA_CLS*1.15*cls_scale`, `ALPHA_PRES*pres_scale`) are known Python floats before the backward, so `differentiating (w_cls*L_cls + w_pres*L_pres)` once should be the same vector in exact arithmetic — and would save one full decoder backward (~1.5× on the whole eval). But `z0_hat_g` is fp16 (the whole trajectory is fp16 from the encoder onward), so the decoder backward runs in fp16 too. Folding the weights into the loss scales the *entire* backward chain before those fp16 intermediates round, rather than after — and with `ALPHA_PRES=0.05`, the preservation branch loses several bits of headroom against fp16's subnormal floor. I measured this directly against the real SD1.5 decoder and the rsna DenseNet on realistic latents: 0.6%–4.7% max relative deviation in the resulting `action` vector across cls/pres scale settings, against an fp16 machine epsilon of 0.1%. That's a real trajectory change, not rounding noise — so I kept the two-backward form and left a comment that fusing requires either moving the guidance block to fp32 or adding explicit gradient-scaling, plus re-validating CV/FID against a prior run either way.

**23. Background lock vs. masked guidance.**
Masked guidance (multiplying `action` by `edit_mask` before adding it to `z_t`) only gates the *correction gradient* — it steers where the classifier/preservation gradients are allowed to push, but the free SDEdit denoising of a high-strength initialization can still repaint the whole thorax on its own, independent of guidance. The background lock is a separate, harder mechanism: at every denoising step, everything outside `edit_mask` is overwritten with the original latent re-noised to the current timestep (`z_bg`), forcing the out-of-lung region onto the identity trajectory by construction, not by penalty. On the final step, the lock uses the *clean* anchor latent, pinning out-of-lung output to `x`'s VAE reconstruction exactly. You need both because guidance alone doesn't structurally prevent drift — it just discourages it — while the lock alone (with `cam_scale=None`) would still let classifier guidance push arbitrarily hard inside the (unconstrained) lung region.

**24. Guidance ramp.**
`guidance_ramp_start_frac` sets where, as a fraction of the DDIM trajectory, guidance starts linearly ramping from 0 to full strength — `guidance_weight = clamp((i - ramp_start_idx) / ramp_span, 0, 1)`. The original design applied guidance as a hard on/off switch at the trajectory midpoint. A discontinuous jump in guidance strength mid-trajectory risked kicking a denoising step into a noisy, unstable region — a smooth ramp avoids that discontinuity while still concentrating guidance in the later, more structure-committed part of the trajectory (never before 10% through, per the action's [0.1, 0.5] range on `ramp_start_frac_t`).

**25. The anchoring bug.**
`environment.step()` chains `generate_counterfactual` over `n_passes`, and `self.x_t` carries across all `BUDGET_T=25` steps of an episode — so with the old default (`x_anchor=None`, which defaults to `x`, the current call's input), the background lock and preservation targets were held against the *previous call's output*, not the episode's original image. Over up to ~100 chained encode/renoise/denoise/decode round trips per episode, this let diffuse change accumulate that no spatial mask constrained, because each individual call looked locally fine relative to its own immediate input. `ANCHOR_TO_EPISODE_ORIGIN` fixes this by passing `x_orig` as `x_anchor` explicitly, so the lock, the segmentation mask, and the preservation L1/KL targets are all held against the ORIGINAL image for the whole episode — `x` remains the SDEdit initialization (so the trajectory still starts from the chained pass input), only the *reference* changes. I kept it as a separate flag from `USE_GRADCAM_MASK` specifically so the two effects (accumulated drift vs. spatial constraint) could be attributed independently in the ablation matrix.

**26. Why multiply the CAM mask into the lung mask rather than replace it?**
The lung segmentation mask is anatomical ground truth — it's derived from a dedicated PSPNet segmenter and defines the physically plausible editable region. Grad-CAM is a coarse saliency estimate that can leak onto the mediastinum, diaphragm, or image border — it's not anatomically constrained the way the segmentation is. Multiplying the two together means the CAM can only ever *tighten* the editable region within what's already anatomically valid, never expand it outside the lung — preserving the existing out-of-lung guarantee from the segmentation mask while adding classifier-attention-based locality on top.

**27. LoRA configuration.**
`LORA_R=8`, `LORA_ALPHA=16`, `LORA_DROPOUT=0.05`, targeting `["to_q", "to_k", "to_v", "to_out.0"]` — the query/key/value/output projections of every cross- and self-attention layer in the UNet. This is the standard LoRA-for-diffusion target-module set; attention layers are where most of the domain-specific adaptation signal concentrates for SD-style models, and leaving the convolutional/ResNet blocks frozen keeps the parameter count tiny. The report states this trains 0.78% of the 866M-parameter generator's parameters — small enough to fit the single-GPU, walltime-bounded HPC budget.

**28. Why upcast LoRA params to fp32 while the base model stays fp16?**
The frozen base UNet/VAE run in fp16 purely for memory/speed — they never receive gradient updates, so precision loss there doesn't compound. The LoRA adapter weights and `cond_proj`, by contrast, ARE being optimized with AdamW — small gradient updates accumulated over many steps are exactly where fp16's limited mantissa causes updates to underflow or lose precision, especially with a modest learning rate. Upcasting just the trainable parameters to fp32 (`build_generator`'s `p.data = p.data.float()`) keeps the optimizer's numerics stable without paying the memory cost of running the whole (frozen) 866M-parameter model in fp32.

**29. What does `cond_proj` do?**
SD1.5's UNet expects cross-attention conditioning shaped like CLIP text-embeddings (77 tokens × 768-d), not a scalar target class or an image latent. `cond_proj` is a small trainable MLP (`Linear → SiLU → Linear`) that maps a concatenation of the target class scalar and a pooled/downsampled version of the original latent (`psi_small`, adaptive-pooled from the full latent) into a single token at `cross_attn_dim`, unsqueezed to shape `(B, 1, cross_attn_dim)`. That single learned token is what the UNet actually attends to instead of a text prompt — it's trained jointly with the LoRA adapters during the denoising-loss fine-tuning phase (`compute_training_losses`), so the model learns to associate that conditioning signal with "generate/complete this image as class c."

## 4. Grad-CAM Action-Space Constraint

**30. Post-hoc penalty vs. hard constraint.**
A post-hoc penalty (e.g. adding a term to the reward that punishes diffuse edits) only discourages the behavior statistically — the policy can still learn to accept the penalty if the flip reward outweighs it, which given the reward's flip-heavy pre-rebalance weighting is exactly what happened. A hard action-space constraint physically bounds what the generator *can* produce: `cam_scale` is multiplied into `edit_mask`, which gates both the guidance gradient magnitude and the background-lock composite, so the output latent deviation per location is mathematically bounded by the mask value there — regardless of what reward the policy is chasing. I chose the hard constraint because the diffuse-editing failure mode was structural (it survived reward rebalancing), so I wanted a mechanism that couldn't be reward-hacked around.

**31. Why compute Grad-CAM once per episode, not per step?**
One densenet121 forward+backward is negligible against the ~1250–4500 UNet evaluations a single episode runs (25 steps × up to 5 refinement passes × up to 50 DDIM timesteps each). Recomputing it per PPO step or per refinement pass would be pure wasted compute for essentially no signal gain, and — more importantly — it would let the mask drift off the *original* image as `x_t` changes within the episode, which defeats the purpose of anchoring the constraint to what the classifier actually attends to on the real input.

**32. `DegenerateCAMError` and why not fall back to a uniform mask.**
Grad-CAM goes spatially constant when the classifier's output has saturated to exactly 0.0 or 1.0 in fp32 — once `p` rounds to an extreme, `d(prob)/d(activations)` is identically zero everywhere, so every location gets the same (zero) contribution. I explicitly detect this via `cam_max <= 0` after ReLU, before normalization — because normalization is exactly what turns a constant zero map into a plausible-looking uniform mask at `floor`. If I let that pass through, the run would *complete normally* and look like a valid "+gradcam_mask" arm while carrying zero localization signal — the most damaging failure mode because it's silent. Instead, `compute_gradcam_latent_mask` returns `None`, which the caller falls back to the lung-only mask for, and — critically — this is *counted* (`_cam_episodes_degenerate`, `_cam_full_degenerate`, `_cam_partial_degenerate`) so a run with a high degeneracy rate can be flagged as not a clean ablation arm rather than silently misreported.

**33. Why "features" specifically, not the last Conv2d?**
DenseNet concatenates every dense layer's output onto a growing channel stack — a single internal dense-layer Conv2d only contributes a narrow slice (e.g. 32 of ~1024) of the channels that actually reach the classifier's final decision. Hooking one internal conv produces a near-uniform map regardless of prediction confidence, because you're only seeing a small fraction of what the classifier is actually using. The "features" submodule is the full concatenated stack right before global pooling — the standard DenseNet Grad-CAM target — so `find_target_layer` prefers it by name and only falls back to the last-spatial-Conv2d heuristic for architectures (like ResNet-18) that don't expose a dedicated features submodule.

**34. Why forward hook + `retain_grad()`, not a backward hook?**
TorchXRayVision's DenseNet `forward` runs `F.relu(features, inplace=True)` on the exact tensor a `register_full_backward_hook` would need to wrap, and PyTorch forbids registering a full backward hook on a tensor that's about to be modified in-place downstream — it breaks autograd's versioning assumptions. `retain_grad()` has no custom-autograd-Function wrapping around the tensor, so the downstream in-place ReLU is handled by ordinary autograd versioning without conflict. I capture the activation via a plain forward hook, call `retain_grad()` on it, run the backward normally, then read `.grad` off the retained tensor.

**35. Why is passing the ensemble directly to `find_target_layer` dangerous?**
`find_target_layer` walks `named_modules()` and returns the first submodule literally named "features" — on an `EnsemblePneumoniaClassifier`, that resolves to `members.0.model.features`, i.e. only the *first* ensemble member (`rsna`, the single-corpus 2-head model I already argued is the easiest to fool). It doesn't raise or warn — it prints its usual success message and returns a real, working layer. The result is a one-member CAM silently presented as "the ensemble's," computed from exactly the model most prone to the shortcut the CAM mask exists to guard against. `ensemble_grad_cam` avoids this by explicitly iterating `ensemble.members`, CAM'ing each against its own forward pass and its own features layer, then averaging the normalized maps (post-normalization averaging so no member's raw activation magnitude dominates).

**36. Why did the Grad-CAM fix hurt so badly (d=1.205, CV 0.838→0.017)?**
[The report identifies this as an open diagnostic item rather than a fully resolved root cause.] What is established: Grad-CAM was degenerate for one of the two ensemble members essentially every episode in the original 7×7-resolution setup, meaning the "ensemble" CAM mask was effectively single-member for most of training. The finer target layer tried in the later diagnosis (`features.denseblock3`, 14×14 resolution) "looked promising in an offline probe" but collapsed CV when trained for real. My working hypothesis is that a finer, noisier saliency map at that resolution — especially when computed from a partially-degenerate ensemble — produces a spatial constraint that's tighter than the anatomically real region where a semantically valid edit needs to happen, so the policy is denied the room to make a real edit and instead either can't reach validity at all or is pushed toward whatever narrow slice the noisy CAM does leave open. This is flagged in the report as the most concrete open item for future diagnosis, not a solved question.

## 5. Classifier Design and Ensemble

**37. Why replace ResNet-18 with XRV DenseNet-121?**
The original custom binary ResNet-18 classifier's `generate_and_plot_diff` output showed diffuse, whole-thorax changes rather than localized opacity synthesis — a visual signature consistent with the classifier having learned a shortcut correlation (e.g. global brightness/contrast/exposure) rather than localized pneumonia-specific features. A classifier trained jointly across many pathologies (TorchXRayVision's DenseNet-121, forced to discriminate pneumonia from effusion, cardiomegaly, edema, etc. simultaneously) is much less able to rely on a single global shortcut, since that shortcut wouldn't help distinguish between the other conditions it also has to get right. It also required zero fine-tuning — a pretrained forward pass, the same "free lunch" pattern later reused for the support-device detector.

**38. The "dead head" NaN problem.**
TorchXRayVision's `op_norm` returns a constant 0.5 with zero gradient wherever a pathology's `op_threshs` entry is NaN — meaning the head can be structurally *present* in the model's output layer but functionally dead: `f(x)` stops depending on the image at all for that pathology. This would be catastrophic for guidance, since `grad_cls` would be identically zero and classifier guidance would silently do nothing while looking like it's running normally. `XRVPneumoniaClassifier.__init__` checks `op_threshs[pna_idx]` for NaN at construction time and raises immediately if found, rather than letting a dead gradient propagate silently through training.

**39. Ensemble rationale and head-count argument.**
Held-out validation showed the single-RSNA-guided counterfactuals transferred very poorly to independent classifiers (transfer ratio 0.13–0.20). Adversarial perturbations rarely survive across independently-trained models with different decision surfaces — requiring several models to agree turns "fool the classifier" into a much narrower target than "make a semantic edit," because an edit has to fool all of them simultaneously. The head-count argument makes this concrete: `-rsna` exposes only 2 output heads (Pneumonia, Lung Opacity), meaning its whole decision surface is built around distinguishing just those two states from everything else — a small, low-dimensional target that's comparatively easy to find an exploit for. `-chex`/`-mimic_ch` expose 11 heads, `-pc` 15, `-nih` 14 — each of those models' Pneumonia head sits inside a much higher-dimensional multi-task decision boundary, constrained by having to simultaneously separate pneumonia from many other conditions, which structurally leaves less room for a narrow adversarial shortcut.

**40. Mean-probability + single BCE vs. mean-of-per-member-BCE.**
`EnsemblePneumoniaClassifier.forward` returns `mean(member outputs)`, and `generate_counterfactual` takes BCE of that mean, not a mean of per-member BCE losses. Under BCE-of-the-mean, if one member's probability is already saturated near the target (small individual gradient, since sigmoid saturates) while another member is lagging, most of the gradient signal through the mean naturally flows to whichever member's output is furthest from saturation — the lagging member — because that's where the loss landscape still has slope. This matters when members disagree substantially: it means guidance implicitly prioritizes convincing whichever classifier is hardest to convince at that moment, rather than applying uniform pressure regardless of how close each member already is. The `per_member` method exists as a diagnostic hook if mean-of-per-member-BCE turns out to be needed instead — [not something I validated empirically as better or worse; kept as an available alternative].

**41. What would resolve `nih` for the 3-model ensemble?**
The report states the 3-member ensemble (`rsna, chex, mimic_nb`) improved `pc` transfer for real but left `nih` "statistically unresolved at this sample size" — LHR lift was inconclusive with n=12 on the reduced leak-free validator set. The direct lever is more samples: a larger paired evaluation set would shrink the confidence interval enough to distinguish a real small effect from noise. [Beyond sample size, whether a structurally different intervention — e.g. actually including an `nih`-family model in the guidance ensemble rather than just holding it out as a validator — would resolve it faster is not something I tested; it's plausible but speculative.]

## 6. Metrics and What They Actually Measure

**42. Why both "moved by τ" AND "crossed the boundary" for CV?**
Requiring only a large move (moved ≥ τ=0.8) without the boundary-crossing check would count an image that started at f(x)=0.05 and moved to f(x_c)=0.85 as valid even though it started nowhere near the boundary and the move, while large, might reflect an unstable rather than a decisively-flipped classification. Requiring only boundary-crossing without the margin would count a marginal 0.49→0.51 flip as fully valid, which is exactly the kind of borderline, easily-reversible result you don't want to credit as a confident counterfactual. Requiring both means the counterfactual has to move decisively AND end up unambiguously on the other side — this is the paper-aligned (Singla et al. 2022) criterion I implemented in `counterfactual_validity`, stricter than either condition alone.

**43. FID backbone change and the normalization bug.**
I moved FID's feature extractor from ImageNet-pretrained Inception-v3 to TorchXRayVision DenseNet-121 penultimate features (1024-d) because it's a domain-matched feature space directly sensitive to pneumonia-relevant visual patterns, unlike Inception's natural-image features. Separately, I found and fixed a real bug in the Inception path (`_InceptionFeatureExtractor`): it was feeding raw [0,1] pixel values with no mean/std normalization, when Inception-v3 was trained on ImageNet-normalized inputs. That pushes every activation off-distribution and inflates FID for *every* comparison, including real-vs-real pairs that should score near zero. Fixed with the standard torchvision `Normalize(mean=[0.485,0.456,0.406], std=[0.229,0.224,0.225])`.

**44. The `eps*I` regularization before `sqrtm`.**
With N samples (typically ~200 after filtering into Xn/Xp groups) less than the 1024 or 2048 feature dimensions, the empirical covariance matrices `sigma1`/`sigma2` are rank-deficient — you literally cannot span a 1024-d or 2048-d covariance from 200 samples, so they're singular by construction. `scipy.linalg.sqrtm` on a near-singular product `sigma1 @ sigma2` is numerically unstable — near-zero eigenvalues produce spurious imaginary or unbounded-magnitude components in the matrix square root. Adding `eps * I` to each covariance diagonal before `sqrtm` regularizes them away from exact singularity, which is the same fix used by the standard `pytorch-fid` reference implementation. I still check for and warn on residual imaginary components above 1e-3 after the fix, since that signals the estimate may still be unreliable and increasing `n_eval` or `eps` is the recommended next step.

**45. FOP's three implementation attempts.**
v1 thresholded the raw feature-vector L2 norm of a YOLOv8 backbone against `fo_threshold=0.5` — but those norms are routinely tens-to-hundreds in magnitude, so the threshold was true for essentially every image regardless of content, giving a degenerate always-1.0 score. v2 fixed the threshold-space issue by switching to the full YOLO detection head instead of a raw feature norm, but `yolov8n.pt` is COCO-pretrained on everyday objects and has never seen a medical support device — so the detection head, while thresholded sensibly, wasn't detecting the right thing at all. v3 tried replacing YOLO entirely with TorchXRayVision's dedicated, CheXpert-trained "Support Devices" head — a properly domain-matched detector requiring zero fine-tuning — but no shipped TorchXRayVision weight set actually exposes that head in practice (it exists in xrv's dataset label-loading code, not in any released weights). `_XRVSupportDeviceDetector.available` checks for this and returns `None`/N/A rather than crashing, and FOP is reported explicitly as "not computable offline" in the final results, rather than silently omitted or faked.

**46. Identity Preservation as FOP's substitute.**
FOP's actual purpose is measuring "is patient-specific, non-pathology content (hardware, anatomy) preserved between the real image and its counterfactual." IP measures that same underlying property without needing any detector model or download: MS-SSIM captures global structural/anatomical identity across multiple spatial scales, and HF-corr — the Pearson correlation of high-pass-filtered image pairs — is a direct proxy for whether thin, high-contrast structures (tubes, leads, pacemaker wires) survive editing, since those are exactly the spatial-frequency content a Gaussian high-pass isolates. I introduced it rather than leaving FOP blank because "not computable" without an alternative would have left preservation entirely unmeasured; IP measures a related but not identical property and is reported as such, not as a drop-in equivalent to the paper's FOP.

**47. Edit concentration.**
`edit_concentration` computes what fraction of total `|x_cf - x|` mass is held by the top 5% of changed pixels, per image, averaged over the group. A perfectly spatially uniform edit distributes its mass evenly across all pixels, so its top-5% share is exactly 5% (`top_frac`) — that's the calibrated noise floor. On synthetic calibration pairs, a diffuse edit changing 75% of pixels at MAE 0.037 (close to what this pipeline actually measured) scored 0.067 — barely above the uniform floor — while a compact 12×12 patch scored 1.000. My v1 counterfactuals scored 0.282 (n→p) / 0.297 (p→n) — meaningfully above the 0.05 floor (so not purely uniform noise) but far below what a genuinely focal, lesion-like edit should score (well above 0.5). That "partly localized but mostly diffuse" signature — a concentrated core sitting on top of a diffuse, low-amplitude tail spread over most of the image — is the quantitative fingerprint of an adversarial perturbation rather than a compact lesion synthesis.

**48. Lesion Hit Rate and why it's "the metric that matters."**
Every other metric in the suite is graded by a model — CV asks a classifier, FID asks a feature extractor, CTR/SCP ask a segmenter and an object-detector backbone. All of them can be satisfied by an edit that changes the right *numbers* for the wrong reason, which is exactly what the held-out validation caught: a system that scored near-perfect CV against its own guidance model transferred at 0-8% to an independent one. LHR asks a human instead — it measures the share of the edit's absolute-difference mass that falls inside a radiologist-drawn opacity annotation (RSNA ships 9,555 such boxes), against the annotation's area share as the chance baseline (`lift = hit_rate / null`; lift=1.0 means indistinguishable from an edit that ignores the annotation entirely). No classifier can be fooled into agreeing with a bounding box a radiologist actually drew. LHR is only defined for p→n because that direction starts from a *positive* image, which has a real annotation to measure against — the edit is supposed to remove what's inside that box. n→p starts from a negative image with no annotation, because there's no finding to draw a box around — computing an LHR-equivalent for n→p would mean inventing a target for "where should the synthesized opacity go," which isn't a well-posed ground-truth question, so `run_full_evaluation` reports n→p as not-applicable for this metric rather than fabricating a number.

**49. SCP as a weak instrument.**
`compute_scp` proxies "healthy costophrenic recess" using a YOLOv8 feature-norm response in the lower-lobe region — low feature norm is interpreted as absence of blunting (fluid). This is a COCO-pretrained backbone that has never seen a chest X-ray or a costophrenic recess specifically; it's a repurposed general-object feature extractor, not a dedicated detector. In the results, SCP's deltas are around 10⁻⁴ against a standard deviation near 10⁻³ — the effect size is essentially noise-scale. The p=0.0042 significance for the n→p SCP delta reflects the paired design and n≈200 (a paired t-test can detect tiny, consistent shifts at that sample size) rather than any shift large enough to represent a real, clinically interpretable change in costophrenic appearance. I flag this explicitly in the results rather than letting a significant p-value be mistaken for a meaningful clinical finding.

**50. Why paired t-tests on within-image delta for CTR/SCP?**
Comparing group means independently (real-group mean vs. counterfactual-group mean as two unpaired samples) throws away the fact that each counterfactual is generated *from* a specific real image — you have a natural pairing, and the interesting question is "did THIS image's CTR/SCP change," not "is the counterfactual group's average different from the real group's average" (which conflates within-image change with between-image variance). A paired test on the delta directly measures the quantity of interest and has much more statistical power at a given sample size, since it removes the substantial inter-patient variance in absolute CTR/SCP values from the comparison.

## 7. Comparison to Prior Work

**51. What was inherited vs. modified from Singla et al. (2022)?**
Inherited exactly: the CV definition (Eq. 11 — margin + boundary-crossing), the FID definition (Eq. 10), the FOP definition (Eq. 12, even though it proved uncomputable and had to be substituted), and the Xn/Xp group-splitting protocol (`f(x)<0.2` / `f(x)>0.8`, n_eval up to 200 per group). Modified: FID's feature backbone (XRV DenseNet instead of the paper's presumed ImageNet backbone, since chest X-rays are domain-mismatched with ImageNet features), and the addition of metrics the paper doesn't have at all — IP (FOP's practical replacement), edit concentration, and Lesion Hit Rate, all added because the held-out validation result showed the paper's original metric suite alone wasn't sufficient to catch an adversarial failure mode.

**52. Architectural comparison to GANterfactual.**
GANterfactual is a conditional GAN trained end-to-end to produce counterfactuals directly, with a fixed generation process determined entirely by training. My pipeline separates "can generate realistic chest X-rays" (SD1.5 + LoRA, trained once via denoising loss) from "how to guide generation toward a target class while preserving identity" (the PPO-controlled gradient-guidance schedule at inference/rollout time). PPO buys adaptivity a fixed schedule wouldn't have: the per-step guidance strength, preservation counter-pressure, number of refinement passes, and ramp timing can all vary based on the current state of the image and how close it already is to the target (`d_t` is part of the observation) — so the policy can, in principle, learn to push harder early on far images and ease off near convergence, rather than applying one static schedule to every image regardless of its starting distance from the boundary.

**53. What does the CycleGAN comparison isolate?**
"Vanilla CycleGAN" has no classifier guidance and no background lock — it's an unguided image-to-image translation baseline. Comparing against it isolates the contribution of guidance + spatial locking as a whole: it reaches the lowest CV of the three compared methods (mean 0.385) and the least realistic images (FID 55.0/45.0), which tells you that just having *a* generative translation model is not sufficient — you need the gradient-guided targeting to reliably hit the classifier boundary, and you need some structural mechanism to keep the output anatomically plausible/realistic (which the background lock and LoRA domain-adaptation both contribute to). Neither piece is optional in the final comparison.

**54. What does the n→p vs. p→n asymmetry between GANterfactual and this system suggest?**
GANterfactual reaches marginally higher CV in n→p (0.998 vs. 0.980) — the "add a pathology" direction — but trails clearly in p→n (0.872 vs. 0.950) — "remove a pathology." My system's advantage in p→n, combined with it also being markedly more realistic in both directions (FID 4-6× better), suggests the gradient-guided approach with explicit preservation pressure is comparatively better suited to *removing* signal cleanly than a fixed GAN mapping is — plausibly because removal requires more careful control over what to leave behind (the preservation term is directly optimizable per-step in my pipeline, whereas a GAN's mapping is fixed at training time and can't adapt its preservation trade-off per image).

**55. Why no baseline until late, and how do I defend the earlier "no baseline" chapters?**
The internship's early priority, once the held-out validation result landed, was resolving whether the *system itself* was doing something real before positioning it against prior work — there's limited value benchmarking a pipeline against baselines if its headline number is later shown to be substantially circular. Chapter 4 states the "no baseline comparison" limitation explicitly and honestly rather than silently omitting it, which is itself part of the project's stated discipline of not rounding a hopeful number up to a demonstrated one. The baseline comparison was added once the ensemble-guided default was validated as the strongest of the internal arms, so it could be compared against prior work under conditions where the comparison meant something rather than comparing an already-known-flawed configuration.

## 8. Validation Integrity — the Central Finding

**56. The held-out validation result, precisely.**
Guidance CV (computed with the same `-rsna` classifier used for guidance) was 0.995 (n→p) / 0.885 (p→n). Re-scoring the identical 200 counterfactuals per direction with two classifiers that never participated in guidance — `-chex` and `-nih` — gave CV of 0.080/0.000 and 0.000/0.000 respectively. This isn't "the model is imperfect" because the drop isn't graceful degradation — it's a near-total collapse from "almost every counterfactual accepted" to "almost none accepted," and in three of four held-out cells, exactly zero. That pattern — success against the exact function you optimised, near-total failure against functions you didn't — is the signature of overfitting to (or exploiting) that specific function, not evidence of a merely-imperfect but genuinely-working system.

**57. Why the agreement control makes this conclusive.**
The agreement control scores the SAME classifiers (`-chex`, `-nih`) against the guidance classifier on the *original, unedited* images — do they broadly agree on which real images are pneumonia-positive? They do, about 70% of the time (0.697, 0.707) — Spearman-style agreement, confirming they share a working notion of the task in general. This rules out the alternative explanation "these validators just disagree with the guidance model in general, so their low scores on counterfactuals don't mean anything specific." If they agreed 70% of the time on real images but 0-8% of the time on the counterfactuals specifically, the failure is localized to the generated images — they're failing to recognize pneumonia specifically in what my system produced, not failing to recognize pneumonia in general.

**58. Transfer ratio and the p→n "fooling, not removing" conclusion.**
Transfer ratio is the fraction of the guidance model's score *shift* that a held-out validator reproduces — i.e., of however much I moved `f_rsna(x)`, how much of that same directional movement does an independent classifier also see. n→p transfer ratios were 0.205/0.590 — meaningfully above zero, meaning some real fraction of the score shift the independent classifiers agree with. p→n transfer ratios were only 0.132/0.143 — both directions of held-out agreement collapse much further. Combined with p→n's held-out CV being literally 0.000 against both validators (versus n→p's non-zero 0.080 against `-chex`), the evidence specifically implicates p→n as the direction where the "edit" is closer to a pure adversarial perturbation against `f_rsna` — moving that one function's score without moving the underlying disease signal that independent models are actually keying on.

**59. The denominator bug.**
[The precise mechanics of the fix are documented at a higher level than full line-by-line code in what I reviewed, but the substance is clear from the report.] The held-out validation code built its held-out ensemble classifier's baseline/denominator regardless of whether ensemble guidance was actually turned on at generation time for the counterfactuals being scored — so the transfer-ratio denominator was computed under a mismatched assumption about the generation configuration. This inflated every previously published transfer ratio; the corrected NIH n→p ratio moved from 0.117 to 0.085. It wasn't caught immediately because the bug produces a plausible-looking, internally-consistent number rather than an obviously-wrong one — the kind of error that only surfaces when you deliberately audit whether the code path that builds the denominator actually reflects the configuration that produced the numerator. It was compounded by a stale-cache bug: cached shards returned their own stored config regardless of any override passed at re-run time, so two supposedly "corrected" re-runs silently produced byte-identical, still-wrong output until the caching itself was fixed.

**60. The shard-routing bug.**
The reduce stage of the sharded evaluation pipeline computed, printed, and even checked the correct shard directory for each arm — but never actually *passed* that directory into the reading step, so every arm silently fell back to reading the trained arm's shards regardless of which arm it claimed to be reducing. I caught it during the integrity audit by tracing the actual data flow from "directory computed" to "directory used" rather than trusting that a printed/logged path implies it was the path that was read. Undetected, this would have made every control arm — anything meant to demonstrate "this technique does nothing" or "this is the baseline" — report metrics numerically identical to the trained arm, because they'd literally be scoring the same underlying tensors. A control arm that can never differ from the treatment arm is worse than no control arm at all, because it looks like confirmation.

**61. The comma-truncated ensemble roster bug.**
The 3-member ensemble roster (`[rsna, chex, mimic_nb]`) was passed to a SLURM job through an environment variable using comma-delimited string encoding. SLURM's env-var passing mechanism split on commas somewhere in the pipeline, truncating the 3-member string down to just its first element — so a run intended to test a 3-model ensemble was silently running with 1 model. What caught it: the resulting Counterfactual Validity matched the single-classifier baseline arm to four decimal places (0.4350 vs. what turned out to be the same 0.3900...-family number) — a coincidence far too precise to be two genuinely different configurations converging independently; it's the signature of the same model being scored twice under different labels. Fixed by reading ensemble membership from the shard's own persisted stamp (written at generation time, not re-parsed from a fragile delimited string) and passing the roster through environment variables in a way that survives SLURM's env handling.

**62. The hand-altered FID values.**
Two recorded FID values in the project's own serialized results — `103.8349346109803` and `59.730386426776136` — appeared in an earlier artifact as `13.8349346109803` and `9.730386426776136`. The discrepancies are exactly −90.0 and −50.0, with ALL 13 mantissa digits preserved identically after the offset. I concluded this was a manual edit rather than a computation bug specifically because of that mantissa preservation — a genuine re-computation (different sample, different seed, different code path) would not reproduce 13 matching trailing digits by chance; the pattern is the unmistakable signature of someone deleting a leading digit by hand from a number that read as inconveniently high, leaving everything after it untouched. I restored both values to freshly recomputed numbers. The lesson this taught me: a project's own historical artifacts — even ones I generated myself — are not automatically trustworthy just because they're "already computed"; if a favorable-looking number in your own records can't be reproduced by rerunning the pipeline that supposedly produced it, that discrepancy itself is a finding worth chasing down, not a detail to wave past.

**63. `test_invariants.py`.**
It's a CPU-only, ~1-minute suite that pins the structural invariants the rest of the pipeline depends on silently holding: train/test split disjointness and that the split is patient-level (not image-level, which would leak the same patient's other views across the boundary), group-scan/index alignment (that `scan_groups`' returned indices actually correspond to the tensors returned alongside them — the exact contract `lesion_hit_rate` depends on), reproduction of LHR calibration numbers, detection of degenerate metrics (e.g. a metric that always returns the same value regardless of input, like the old FOP), and refusal to merge shards from mixed ablation arms into one lossless partition. It matters specifically because the real compute — SLURM jobs, multi-hour training runs — is expensive and slow to iterate on; running this suite on the login node in under a minute catches an entire class of "the pipeline plumbing is silently wrong" bugs (index misalignment, config drift, split leakage) before burning a 24-hour GPU allocation on a run whose output would have been invalid from the start.

**64. Why the persisted patient-level split, and what was wrong before it existed?**
Before `splits.py`, a single shared dataset object was fed to LoRA fine-tuning, the PPO environment's far-pool (the images the policy does rollouts against), and the metrics evaluation scan alike, with no restriction. That means every headline number reported before this existed was measured, at least partly, on images the generator had literally been fine-tuned on and the policy had practised generating counterfactuals from — the evaluation set and the training set overlapped. A patient-level split matters specifically because RSNA (like most CXR datasets) can have multiple images per patient; splitting by image alone can put two views of the same patient on opposite sides of the boundary, which still leaks patient-specific information across the split. `splits.py` builds and persists a single, patient-level train/test partition that every downstream consumer (`environment.py`'s `allowed_indices`, `metrics.scan_groups`'s `allowed_indices`) is required to respect, so a counterfactual reported at eval time is guaranteed to be generated for an image the policy never trained or rolled out on.

## 9. Failure Modes and Limitations

**65. Is "you just fooled your own classifier" fair as a characterization of the whole project?**
It's a fair and accurate characterization of the pre-ensemble numbers reported in Chapter 4 — CV 0.975 with the single-`rsna` guidance classifier really was substantially, though not entirely, a case of an adversarial perturbation rather than a semantic edit, and I say so directly in the report ("the p→n direction in particular was not removing disease, it was fooling f(·)"). It's not a fair characterization of the full body of work, because the later chapters build and validate a real (if partial) countermeasure: ensemble guidance is shown, across three independent seeds, to reliably improve lesion targeting with a small-to-medium effect (Cohen's d≈0.15-0.22), improves held-out mean transfer on both directions when policies are actually retrained under it (0.230→0.282 n→p, 0.146→0.293 p→n), and beats both a Vanilla CycleGAN and GANterfactual baseline under the identical protocol. But `nih` — the most independent single validator — still doesn't improve on n→p, staying in the adversarial range even under the ensemble default. So the honest answer is: substantially true of the earliest numbers, partially but not completely resolved by the end.

**66. Why leave the dead `pres_suppression_floor` in the shipped config?**
Given `guidance_bias`'s actual sigmoid range (0.70, 1.30), `1/guidance_bias` only ever reaches (0.769, 1.428) — the 0.6 floor mathematically never binds at current action bounds. I left it in rather than deleting it because it's cheap insurance against a *future* change: if the guidance-bias action range is ever widened (which the report itself recommends as future work, since both policies saturate at the current range's extremes), the floor would then actually start constraining behavior, and removing it now would mean re-deriving and re-adding that safety margin later, at a point where it's less visible that it's needed. It's documented explicitly as inert today in the code comment, so nobody mistakes it for an active constraint when reading the reward/action logic — that documentation is the part that actually matters, not the presence of the floor itself.

**67. Guidance-bias saturation — policy strategy or bound artifact?**
Both directional policies converge to `guidance_bias` at the two literal extremes of its [0.70, 1.30] range, with standard deviation below 0.006 despite the policy sampling with real exploration noise (std≈0.61) during training. That combination — extreme value, near-zero variance, despite substantial exploration noise — indicates the *action bound* is the binding constraint, not a nuanced learned policy: the policy wants to push guidance_bias even further than the bound allows in each direction, and the sigmoid squash is simply clipping it there. This matters for interpretation because I can't claim "the policy learned that n→p needs guidance-bias≈1.3 specifically" — the honest claim is "the policy wants MORE bias toward classifier guidance than the current range permits, for n→p, and MORE bias toward preservation than the range permits, for p→n." The actual optimal value, if the range were wider, is unknown — this is a concrete, stated item for future work (widen the range and see where it actually settles).

**68. Why N≈200 for FID is a real problem, not just "more data would help."**
FID's variance and bias both depend on how well the finite sample's empirical covariance approximates the true, population covariance in feature space. With N≈200 samples against a 1024-dimensional (XRV) or 2048-dimensional (Inception) feature space, the empirical covariance matrix is rank-deficient by construction — you cannot span more dimensions than you have independent samples. This is why I need the `eps*I` regularization before `sqrtm` at all (Q44) — without it, the near-singular covariance product produces numerically unstable, sometimes complex-valued results. Even with the regularization applied, the resulting FID number's confidence interval is wide; a difference of, say, 10 FID points between two runs at this sample size may not be distinguishable from sampling noise. That's a genuine statistical limitation on how much weight the absolute FID numbers can bear, not merely "it would be nicer to have more data" — it directly determines whether a comparison between two FID numbers is meaningful at all.

**69. What would change if the compute budget doubled?**
`N_EPISODES=800` and `N_DENOISE_EPOCHS=30` are explicitly documented as placeholders sized to fit the available walltime, not values chosen by an ablation that found diminishing returns. [I did not run the doubled-budget experiment, so I can't state what would actually happen — this is honestly unknown.] What I can say is what I'd be checking for: whether CV/FID/LHR are still moving meaningfully with more training (suggesting the current budget under-trains the policy/LoRA adapters) or have plateaued (suggesting the current numbers already reflect close to what this architecture can achieve, and the gaps found — especially the held-out transfer gap — are structural rather than a training-budget artifact). Given that the transfer gap didn't fully close even under the best-performing countermeasure (ensemble guidance), my prior is that at least part of the gap is structural rather than purely a training-budget shortfall, but I'd want the doubled-budget run before stating that with confidence.

**70. Why might sparsity reward have hurt lesion targeting despite mechanically reducing diffuse editing?**
[This is flagged in the conclusion as a "clear negative result" without a fully resolved root cause — I'll give my honest hypothesis, not a confirmed mechanism.] The sparsity term (`R_sparsity`, an L1 penalty optionally weighted outside the CAM region) pushes the policy toward making *smaller total edits* overall — it doesn't know where a "correct" edit should go, only that edits should be smaller in aggregate. If achieving classifier flip under a tighter total-edit budget is easier by finding a small, concentrated, but *anatomically wrong* location — e.g., some non-lesion region the classifier happens to be locally sensitive to — than by making a moderately larger but *correct* edit at the actual lesion site, the sparsity pressure would push the policy toward the former: small and adversarial rather than appropriately sized and semantically correct. That's consistent with the result — the term did what it was mechanically designed to do (reduce total edit magnitude, which the annealed weight and CV safeguard confirm it does without crashing CV) while making localization measurably worse (d=0.512, p=4.7e-9), which suggests "smaller edit" and "better-targeted edit" are not the same objective, and optimizing the former doesn't reliably deliver the latter.

## 10. Infrastructure / Engineering

**71. SLURM setup.**
`run_ppo.slurm` submits to partition `publicgpu` under account `insa`, with `--array=0-1` running one task per direction (n→p, p→n) in parallel, `--gres=gpu:1`, `--cpus-per-task=8`, `--mem=64G`, and a GPU constraint string `"gpuh200|gpuh100|gpua100|gpul40s"` accepting any of several modern GPU families. I explicitly `--exclude=hpc-n983` because that node carries a Blackwell sm_120 GPU whose SLURM feature tag `gpurtx6000` is indistinguishable from genuinely older Turing Quadro RTX 6000 nodes — a job that landed there hit `RuntimeError: CUDA error: no kernel image is available` about 5 minutes into job 17662452_0, because the pinned `torch==2.5.1+cu121` build's compiled kernels stop at sm_90 and have no kernel image for sm_120 at all.

**72. `preflight_gpu.py`.**
It fails fast, before any real work starts, by checking `torch.cuda.get_device_capability()` against `torch.cuda.get_arch_list()` — i.e. confirming the venv's installed torch actually has compiled kernels for whatever GPU SLURM allocated this job, rather than discovering that fact only after loading multi-GB model weights and hitting a cryptic CUDA error partway into the first forward pass. It accepts the whole major SM family rather than an exact match (e.g. an sm_86 binary is valid on an sm_89 Ada L40S card), so it doesn't over-reject compatible-but-not-identical hardware. This directly prevents the class of bug in Q71 — a job burning walltime and (worse) burning the SLURM queue's turnaround time before failing on a hardware/software mismatch that's knowable in the first second of the job.

**73. Why save `cv_pre_ramp`/`lambda_frozen`/`ramp_history` alongside weights on resume?**
These fields carry the entire state of the CV-safeguard/sparsity-ramp mechanism (Q18) — not just "what are the current network weights" but "where in the annealing schedule are we, and has the freeze already latched." If a resume only restored the policy and optimizer weights, `cv_pre_ramp` would reset to `None` and `lambda_frozen` to `None` on the next launch — silently re-baselining the CV probe against a policy that's already mid-ramp (giving a wrong "pre-ramp" reference point) and releasing a freeze that had already correctly latched to protect CV from further degradation. That's precisely the ramp-oscillation failure mode the freeze mechanism exists to prevent — a resumed run could un-freeze and re-degrade CV, and you'd have no way to tell from the final artifact that this had happened partway through.

**74. Why worst-case, not mean, update duration for the wallclock budget check?**
CV-probe updates cost roughly 3× a plain update (they run a full extra deterministic policy rollout over the held-out set). A mean estimate would be dominated by the many cheap ordinary updates and systematically under-predict exactly the updates most at risk of overrunning — the CV-probe updates. Using the slowest update seen so far as the estimate for "will the next update fit" is conservative in the right direction: it might trigger a slightly earlier stop-and-checkpoint than strictly necessary on a run with no probe coming up, but it guarantees the guard never lets a probe-carrying update start when it would actually overrun the walltime and get SIGKILLed with nothing saved.

**75. The concurrent-write race.**
Two directional training processes (n→p and p→n), seeded identically per the seeding scheme, running the same architecture on similar hardware, reached checkpoint-save boundaries within seconds of each other and were writing to the same shared temp checkpoint filename before an atomic `os.replace`. Atomic rename alone protects against a crash mid-write corrupting the target file — it does NOT protect against two *different* processes both writing to the same temp path concurrently, where one process's write can interleave with or clobber the other's before either reaches its own rename, potentially letting a corrupted interleaved result get promoted via a "successful" atomic rename. The fix was giving each process its own uniquely-named temp file (e.g. incorporating a process-specific identifier) before the atomic rename step, so the two processes never share a write target at all.

**76. Job 17653310 as a turning point.**
That job was SIGKILLed at the walltime roughly 30 of 100 planned updates into n→p training, with zero PPO checkpoints written — the entire 24 hours of compute was unrecoverable, and there was no artifact at all to show for it. That directly motivated the resumable checkpointing system (`_save_state`/`_load_state`, `PPO_CKPT_EVERY_N_UPDATES`) and the wallclock-budget guard (Q74) that stops training BEFORE starting an update likely to overrun, saves, and exits cleanly rather than waiting to be killed. It's the single event that reframed infrastructure work from "nice to have" to "without this, a training run's outcome is a coin-flip on whether SLURM's scheduler happens to give you enough contiguous walltime in one submission."

## 11. Ethical / Clinical Implications

**77. Safeguards against a generated image being mistaken for a real diagnostic image?**
[No specific technical safeguard against this — e.g. watermarking, embedded provenance metadata, or a hard block on export in a diagnostic-looking format — is present in the codebase I reviewed.] This is a research pipeline that outputs raw pixel tensors and saves them as PNGs (e.g. `outputs/best_counterfactual_candidates/`) with no visible marking distinguishing a counterfactual from a real acquisition. In a research context with a small, informed team, this risk is low, but it would be a hard requirement before any clinician-facing deployment — I'd flag this as a genuine gap in the current pipeline rather than something already handled.

**78. What does the held-out validation finding imply about trusting single-model-validated claims in clinical ML generally?**
The core lesson — stated as the report's central thesis — is that a metric computed from the same function (or function family) a system was optimized against cannot be trusted as independent evidence of that system's real-world behavior, no matter how favorable the number looks, because the optimization process actively searches for and exploits exactly the properties that inflate that metric. This generalizes well beyond this project: any clinical ML system reporting performance against a metric derived from a model it was trained, fine-tuned, or guided against — rather than against an independently-sourced, independently-computed ground truth (here, radiologist-drawn lesion boxes) — should be treated with the same skepticism I applied to my own CV=0.975 number until an independent check is run. The corollary is a design principle, not just a caution: build the independent check as part of the system, not as an afterthought, and be prepared to report what it finds even when it undermines the headline result.

**79. What would I refuse to ship as a clinician-facing tool today?**
I would refuse to ship the single-classifier-guided configuration (the one behind the Chapter 4 CV=0.975 number) as anything presented to a clinician as evidence of "here's what this pathology would look like" — the held-out validation shows that number substantially reflects an adversarial exploit of one classifier rather than a depiction of real disease signal, and presenting it as clinically meaningful would be actively misleading. I'd also refuse to ship without the LHR-style independent, human-grounded validation running continuously in production as a monitor, not just as a one-time offline check — because the failure mode demonstrated here (a system quietly learning to satisfy its own metric rather than the underlying task) is exactly the kind of drift that can re-emerge after further training or a data distribution shift, and a one-time validation at ship-time wouldn't catch that recurrence.

## 12. Devil's-Advocate / Pressure Questions

**80. "You spent half the internship building infrastructure to catch your own bugs. Wasn't that wasted effort?"**
No — and the report's own framing backs this up directly: several of the bugs the integrity audit caught (the shard-routing bug that would have made every control arm identical to the trained arm, the comma-truncated ensemble roster, the denominator bug that inflated every published transfer ratio) would each individually have invalidated any comparison built on top of them, silently. A result built on a broken control arm isn't a weaker result — it's not evidence at all, and worse, it looks like evidence. The alternative to spending that time was shipping conclusions I had no actual grounds to hold, which isn't a time-savings, it's exporting the cost of finding these bugs onto whoever tries to build on this work later, at a point where the original context for diagnosing them is gone. The report states plainly that "the project's most valuable output is a control that exposed its own primary metric as circular" — that's not a side effect of the internship, in my view it's the central deliverable.

**81. "Isn't `-chex` held-out validation itself compromised once `-chex` joins the guidance ensemble?"**
Yes, and this is exactly why the later chapters explicitly move to the "leak-free validator set" `{nih, pc, mimic_ch}` for evaluating the ensemble-guided policies, rather than continuing to quote `-chex` as an independent check once it's a guidance member. This is a real methodological constraint I had to actively manage, not something I missed — the report is explicit that once you add a classifier to guidance, it can no longer serve as its own held-out validator, and the validator pool available for any given guidance configuration necessarily shrinks. It's part of why the 3-model ensemble experiment reports scores against the reduced `{nih, pc}` set specifically flagged as "not directly comparable to the other rows' 3-validator mean" in the final comparison table — I tracked and disclosed the comparability boundary rather than letting it blur.

**82. "PPO adds huge complexity for what your own report shows is close to a no-op reward-scaling effect. Why not a simpler fixed schedule?"**
The specific effect that's "close to a no-op" is `direction_scale`, a single uniform multiplier on the whole reward — that particular knob washing out under GAE's advantage normalization doesn't mean the rest of PPO's machinery is doing nothing. The 9-dimensional per-step action space (strength, cls_scale, pres_scale, guidance_bias, ramp timing, extra passes) still gives the policy real per-image, per-step adaptivity that a fixed schedule can't provide — the guidance diagnostics show the two directional policies converge to genuinely different, extreme strategies (guidance_bias at opposite ends of its range) precisely because the two directions need different guidance/preservation trade-offs, which a hand-tuned single schedule would have to compromise between rather than specialize for. That said, this is a fair pressure point on cost-effectiveness: I did NOT run a controlled comparison against a well-tuned fixed schedule as a baseline, so I can't claim PPO's added complexity is empirically justified over that specific simpler alternative — only that the mechanism it provides (state-dependent, per-step control) is doing something a fixed schedule structurally cannot.

**83. "Two of three countermeasures made things worse. Doesn't that suggest the generation mechanism is fundamentally broken?"**
It's a reasonable read, but I don't think the evidence supports "fundamentally broken" over "the failure mode is narrower than initially feared, and two of three fixes were the wrong shape for it." Ensemble guidance — the one countermeasure that helped — attacks the problem at its actual source: the guidance classifier's narrow, exploitable decision surface. The two that hurt attacked different, more indirect proxies for the same symptom: Grad-CAM tried to spatially constrain WHERE edits happen, sparsity tried to constrain HOW MUCH changes overall — neither directly addresses "is the underlying classifier signal being exploited," and both introduced their own failure modes (degenerate CAM computation, small-edit-but-wrong-location incentive). The fact that the countermeasure that actually targets the root cause helped, while two structurally-indirect countermeasures hurt, is more consistent with "the mechanism has one specific, addressable weak point" than with "everything about the approach is broken" — but I hold this as the best-supported read of the evidence, not a certainty; the report is explicit these two negative results remain open diagnostic questions.

**84. "Is a d=0.15-0.22 effect really strong enough to call the ensemble default 'validated'?"**
By itself, no — and I don't think the report claims that. What justifies calling it the default isn't the size of the effect in isolation, it's the effect replicating in the same direction across three independent seeds, combined with it being the only one of five tested internal arms that a paired significance test confirms as an improvement rather than "confirmed worse" or "inconclusive." The report's own table is explicit about grading this on paired tests and confidence intervals rather than point estimates specifically to avoid overstating a small effect — and the conclusion's wording is careful: "a real, partial countermeasure, not a solved problem," not "validated" in the sense of fully closing the transfer gap (which it doesn't — `nih` on n→p remains unresolved even under the ensemble default). Calling it "the strongest technique found" is a comparative, relative claim among the options actually tried and measured under this project's evidentiary bar — not a claim that the transfer problem is solved.
